About zircon

Zircon is its own mineral, not cubic zirconia. Zircon found in Australia is the oldest mineral on Earth, 4.4 billion years old. Very high luster, refractive index and dispersion give it brilliance and fire, and its strong double refraction can show the back facets doubled. Hardness 6 to 7.5 (GIA).

The zircon story

History

  • Many scholars trace the name to the Arabic "zarkun" (cinnabar or vermilion); others to the Persian "zargun," meaning gold colored (GIA).
  • Colorless zircon was used as a diamond look-alike in the early 1900s, long before modern simulants.
  • Blue zircon was a Victorian favorite. George Kunz, Tiffany's famed gem buyer, championed it and proposed the name "starlite." It never caught on.

Cool facts

  • Zircon found in Australia is the oldest mineral on Earth: 4.4 billion years old (GIA).
  • Very high luster, refractive index and dispersion give it lots of brilliance and flashes of fire.
  • Pronounced double refraction: under a loupe you can see the back facets doubled.
  • A birthstone for December, along with turquoise and tanzanite.
  • Zircon weighs more than most gems of the same size.

Family and species

  • Zircon is its own mineral, zirconium silicate (ZrSiO4).
  • Not cubic zirconia, which is a man-made material.
  • Colors include blue, red, yellow, orange, brown and green. Hardness 6 to 7.5 (GIA).

About spinel

The spinel story

History

  • For centuries red and pink spinel was mistaken for ruby. The "Black Prince's Ruby" in the British Imperial State Crown is actually a red spinel.
  • Old mines in Central Asia sent out big crystals known as "Balas rubies."
  • In 1783, mineralogist Jean Baptiste Louis Romé de l'Isle showed that spinel is a different mineral from ruby.

Cool facts

  • Pinks come from traces of chromium; purple tones come from a mix of iron and chromium (GIA).
  • Hardness 8, tough enough for everyday rings.
  • Singly refractive, so the color looks the same from every direction.

Family and species

  • Spinel is its own gem species, a magnesium-aluminum oxide, not a ruby or sapphire.
  • It gives its name to the spinel group of minerals.

About tsavorite

Green grossular garnet, colored by vanadium and chromium. Campbell Bridges found it in Tanzania in 1967 and in Kenya in 1970; it was named tsavorite in 1973 and reached jewelry buyers in 1974. Rarer than red garnet, because it needs unusual rock chemistry, and most stones are small. Singly refractive, often red through a Chelsea filter, hardness 7 to 7.5. As a species it generally isn't treated; that's about the variety, not any one stone.

The tsavorite story

History

  • Geologist Campbell Bridges found it in northern Tanzania in 1967, then in Kenya's Tsavo National Park in 1970.
  • He won a permit to mine the Kenyan deposit in 1971.
  • The name, given in 1973, honors Tsavo National Park.
  • Gem tsavorite still comes mainly from the Kenya–Tanzania border. It has also been documented in Madagascar.

Cool facts

  • The finest are intense green to blue-green, and can pass for emerald at a glance.
  • Refractive index about 1.74, and no facet doubling, so the facet edges stay crisp.
  • Look inside and you may see feathers of tiny negative crystals, fluid inclusions or fine fibers.
  • It usually comes small, so value climbs fast with size. Cutters favor shapes that save weight from the rough.
  • Garnet is the birthstone for January and the gem for the second anniversary.

Family and species

  • Garnet group → grossular → tsavorite.
  • GIA counts more than 20 garnet species. Five matter as gems: pyrope, almandine, spessartine, grossular and andradite. A sixth, uvarovite, is usually too small to cut.
  • Grossular may have the widest color range of any garnet, from colorless through yellow and reddish orange to a strong, vibrant green. Tsavorite and hessonite are both grossular.
  • Its green cousin demantoid belongs to another species, andradite. Like tsavorite, it's usually found small.

About tourmaline

Not one mineral but a whole family of borosilicates; elbaite gives us most gem tourmaline, and liddicoatite is named for Richard T. Liddicoat. One of the widest color ranges of any gem: one pegmatite pocket can turn up several colors, and one crystal can grow pink at the core and green at the rim. Manganese is what makes it pink. Doubly refractive and strongly pleochroic, so the color shifts as you turn it. Hardness 7 to 7.5; mined worldwide, from Brazil to Namibia and Russia (GIA).

The tourmaline story

History

  • The name comes from the Sinhalese "toramalli", meaning "mixed gems". Dutch merchants used it for the many-colored pebbles in the gem gravels of Ceylon, now Sri Lanka.
  • In 1554, Francisco Spinoza's expedition in Brazil found a "Brazilian emerald": the first recorded green tourmaline crystal.
  • For centuries it passed as ruby, sapphire or emerald. Scientists recognized tourmaline as its own mineral species in the 1800s.
  • In the late 1800s, gemologist George F. Kunz championed the tourmalines of Maine and California. China's Dowager Empress Tz'u Hsi loved the pinks, and San Diego County's pink and red crystals were shipped to China for carving until 1912.
  • Liddicoatite is named for Richard T. Liddicoat, former president of GIA, often called the "Father of Modern Gemology".

Cool facts

  • The Smithsonian counts more than 14 tourmaline minerals. Only one, elbaite, gives nearly all the gems.
  • October's birthstone, along with opal, and the gem of the eighth anniversary.
  • Look down through the top: the back facets can show doubled, a telltale sign of strong double refraction.
  • Strongly pleochroic. Look down its length, and the color goes darker.
  • Pyroelectric and piezoelectric: heat it or squeeze it, and it builds a small electric charge.
  • Iron, with or without titanium, makes greens and blues. Manganese makes pinks and reds. Vanadium and chromium make intense greens in the magnesium-rich species.

Family and species

  • Tourmaline group, a complex borosilicate. Trigonal crystals.
  • Elbaite (sodium, lithium, aluminum): most gem tourmaline, from granite pegmatites.
  • Liddicoatite (calcium, lithium, aluminum): pegmatites, known for complex color zoning.
  • Dravite and uvite (magnesium-rich): form in metamorphosed limestone and marble.
  • Schorl: black, rich in iron.

The mandarin garnet story

History

  • The "mandarin" name started with vivid orange spessartine found in 1991 near the Kunene River in Namibia.
  • The trade now uses it for any spessartine of that bright orange. Later finds include Loliondo, Tanzania (2007).
  • These are stories about the variety, not this stone's origin.

Cool facts

  • Spessartine is the manganese garnet, and manganese is what makes it orange.
  • Singly refractive, hardness 7 to 7.5.
  • Garnet is the birthstone for January and the gem for the second anniversary.

Family and species

  • Garnet group → spessartine. Mandarin is a trade name for its vivid orange.
  • GIA counts more than 20 garnet species. Five matter as gems: pyrope, almandine, spessartine, grossular and andradite.
  • Spessartine belongs to the pyralspite series, with pyrope and almandine.

Coming soon to EtsyFavorite the shop on Etsy
The Mali garnet story

History

  • Mali garnet first came to market in spring 1994 at Idar-Oberstein, from the Kayes Region of western Mali.
  • GIA's Gems & Gemology described it in 1995. Labs call it "grossular-andradite garnet."
  • The name is a trade name; this stone's mine isn't known.

Cool facts

  • Colors run mostly yellow-green to brown; the andradite (iron) part of the mix adds warm tones.
  • Between crossed polarizers it shows stacked growth planes, a classic ID clue.
  • Singly refractive (it can show some anomalous double refraction), hardness about 7.

Family and species

  • Garnet group → a blend of grossular and andradite, two species of the ugrandite series.
  • Grossular gives us tsavorite and hessonite; andradite gives us demantoid.
