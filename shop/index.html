About tourmaline

Not one mineral but a whole family of borosilicates; elbaite gives us most gem tourmaline, and liddicoatite is named for Richard T. Liddicoat. One of the widest color ranges of any gem: one pegmatite pocket can turn up several colors, and one crystal can grow pink at the core and green at the rim. Iron is what makes it blue to green. Doubly refractive and strongly pleochroic, so the color shifts as you turn it. Hardness 7 to 7.5; mined worldwide, from Brazil to Namibia and Russia (GIA).

The tourmaline story

History

  • The name comes from the Sinhalese "toramalli", meaning "mixed gems". Dutch merchants used it for the many-colored pebbles in the gem gravels of Ceylon, now Sri Lanka.
  • In 1554, Francisco Spinoza's expedition in Brazil found a "Brazilian emerald": the first recorded green tourmaline crystal.
  • For centuries it passed as ruby, sapphire or emerald. Scientists recognized tourmaline as its own mineral species in the 1800s.
  • In the late 1800s, gemologist George F. Kunz championed the tourmalines of Maine and California. China's Dowager Empress Tz'u Hsi loved the pinks, and San Diego County's pink and red crystals were shipped to China for carving until 1912.
  • Liddicoatite is named for Richard T. Liddicoat, former president of GIA, often called the "Father of Modern Gemology".

Cool facts

  • The Smithsonian counts more than 14 tourmaline minerals. Only one, elbaite, gives nearly all the gems.
  • October's birthstone, along with opal, and the gem of the eighth anniversary.
  • Look down through the top: the back facets can show doubled, a telltale sign of strong double refraction.
  • Strongly pleochroic. Look down its length, and the color goes darker.
  • Pyroelectric and piezoelectric: heat it or squeeze it, and it builds a small electric charge.
  • Iron, with or without titanium, makes greens and blues. Manganese makes pinks and reds. Vanadium and chromium make intense greens in the magnesium-rich species.

Family and species

  • Tourmaline group, a complex borosilicate. Trigonal crystals.
  • Elbaite (sodium, lithium, aluminum): most gem tourmaline, from granite pegmatites.
  • Liddicoatite (calcium, lithium, aluminum): pegmatites, known for complex color zoning.
  • Dravite and uvite (magnesium-rich): form in metamorphosed limestone and marble.
  • Schorl: black, rich in iron.

About tsavorite

Green grossular garnet, colored by vanadium and chromium. Campbell Bridges found it in Tanzania in 1967 and in Kenya in 1970; it was named tsavorite in 1973 and reached jewelry buyers in 1974. Rarer than red garnet, because it needs unusual rock chemistry, and most stones are small. Singly refractive, often red through a Chelsea filter, hardness 7 to 7.5. As a species it generally isn't treated; that's about the variety, not any one stone.

The tsavorite story

History

  • Geologist Campbell Bridges found it in northern Tanzania in 1967, then in Kenya's Tsavo National Park in 1970.
  • He won a permit to mine the Kenyan deposit in 1971.
  • The name, given in 1973, honors Tsavo National Park.
  • Gem tsavorite still comes mainly from the Kenya–Tanzania border. It has also been documented in Madagascar.

Cool facts

  • The finest are intense green to blue-green, and can pass for emerald at a glance.
  • Refractive index about 1.74, and no facet doubling, so the facet edges stay crisp.
  • Look inside and you may see feathers of tiny negative crystals, fluid inclusions or fine fibers.
  • It usually comes small, so value climbs fast with size. Cutters favor shapes that save weight from the rough.
  • Garnet is the birthstone for January and the gem for the second anniversary.

Family and species

  • Garnet group → grossular → tsavorite.
  • GIA counts more than 20 garnet species. Five matter as gems: pyrope, almandine, spessartine, grossular and andradite. A sixth, uvarovite, is usually too small to cut.
  • Grossular may have the widest color range of any garnet, from colorless through yellow and reddish orange to a strong, vibrant green. Tsavorite and hessonite are both grossular.
  • Its green cousin demantoid belongs to another species, andradite. Like tsavorite, it's usually found small.
